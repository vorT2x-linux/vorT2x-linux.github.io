AirDrop and Quick Share from Fedora

Kirk19 November 20264 min read

Someone sends you a photo, a boarding pass or a PDF from their phone, and on a Mac it just turns up. Until now that stopped working the moment you booted Linux. Not any more: VorT2x comes with T2 AirShare, which sends and receives files with Android phones over Quick Share and with iPhones, iPads and Macs over AirDrop, straight from Fedora.

It's installed for you, it sits quietly in the background, and it works from the file manager you already use. Here's how to use it, and a peek at what makes it work.

Sending files

Right-click one or more files and choose Send with T2 AirShare. The entry is there in GNOME Files, KDE's Dolphin and Nemo. You can also open T2 AirShare from your app menu and drop files on its window.

A send window opens and starts looking for devices nearby:

  • Android phones show up over Quick Share.
  • iPhones, iPads and Macs show up over AirDrop.
  • A device that offers both appears only once, with AirDrop picked for it.

Click the device you want. With Quick Share, both screens show the same four-digit PIN so you can be sure you picked the right phone. A progress bar follows the transfer until it's done.

Can't see a device? The other device has to be visible to everyone. On an iPhone, open Control Centre, long-press the network controls and set AirDrop to Everyone for 10 Minutes. On Android, set Quick Share visibility to Everyone. Bluetooth also needs to be on, on both sides. If it's off on your Mac, the send window offers to switch it on.

Receiving files

Open T2 AirShare and switch on Visible to nearby devices. Your Mac now appears in the share sheet on Android phones, and as an AirDrop destination on Apple devices nearby. It works on the same Wi-Fi, and over Bluetooth when the phone isn't on your network.

When someone sends you something, a notification asks whether to accept it. Files land in your Downloads folder, or another folder you pick in the app, and the notification gives you a button to open them.

For your own phone, you don't need to say yes every time. Mark a device as trusted from the notification, and its transfers are accepted straight away. You can remove trusted devices again in the app's settings.

How it works

Neither of these is a simple feature to switch on. Both had to be built from the ground up for Linux.

Quick Share

Android phones only start looking for a receiver after they hear a particular Bluetooth Low Energy signal. So T2 AirShare sends that signal, announces itself on your Wi-Fi, then runs the same encrypted handshake an Android phone does before any file moves.

When a phone isn't on your Wi-Fi, the transfer starts over Bluetooth. Bluetooth is slow for big files, so T2 AirShare then opens a small Wi-Fi hotspot just for that phone and moves the transfer onto it, the same way two Android phones do. The hotspot runs alongside your normal Wi-Fi, so you stay connected, and it closes as soon as the transfer is done.

AirDrop

AirDrop is harder. Apple devices don't use your Wi-Fi network for it. They talk directly to each other over AWDL, Apple Wireless Direct Link, a private peer-to-peer Wi-Fi mode that Linux has never had. The Broadcom Wi-Fi chips in T2 Macs support AWDL in their firmware, because macOS relies on it. VorT2x patches Linux's Broadcom driver to switch that firmware support on. A small system service called t2-drop then handles AWDL and speaks AirDrop the way macOS does.

It's built to be a good neighbour:

  • Looking for AirDrop devices keeps AWDL running on the Macs around you, which slows their Wi-Fi a little. So the send window only looks while it's the window you're using.
  • For receiving, your Mac only becomes visible over AirDrop when someone nearby is actually trying to send, and only while you're logged in with the screen unlocked, just like macOS.
  • Everything is switched off before your Mac goes to sleep, and comes back when it's needed.
  • Received files are saved with your own user rights, and file paths that try to escape the download folder are dropped.

From the terminal

Prefer the command line? Both halves have one:

Quick Share, to a phone on the same Wi-Fi whose name contains “Pixel”:

$ t2airshare send --to Pixel photo.jpg

AirDrop, to see who's around and then send to an iPhone:

$ t2drop list
$ t2drop send --to iPhone notes.pdf

Run either with --help to see all the options.

Standing on shoulders

T2 AirShare wouldn't exist without the people who worked out how these protocols behave. The Quick Share side follows the protocol notes from NearDrop and Google's open Nearby code, and adapts parts of rquickshare. Thank you to all of them.

Try it and tell us

AirDrop on Linux is brand new, and T2 Macs come with a few different Wi-Fi chips. If you try it, we'd love to hear how it went, and which Mac and which phone you used. Successes are just as useful as problems. Tell us on GitHub.
