Behind the scenes

Tuning the T2 iMacs for gaming

Kirk23 November 20267 min read

Yesterday I mentioned that my iMac Pro could power off when a game started. That's fixed. In VorT2x v0.2.3 the iMac Pro plays games without going to sleep or switching itself off, thanks to two changes: fans that react to the graphics card instead of waiting for the heat, and a steady limit on the GPU's top clock speed.

Along the way the desktop Macs (iMacs, Mac minis and Mac Pros) also lost three laptop services they never needed. Here's what was going on, how we tracked it down, and what it means for your Mac.

The game that put the Mac to sleep

The symptom was strange. I'd open Counter-Strike 2, click find match, and within seconds the iMac Pro would go to sleep, as if someone had pressed the sleep button. A couple of times it went further and switched off completely: no warning, nothing in the log. Once a game was actually running, it played fine.

The sleep button turned out to be exactly that. The Mac's own power controller, the SMC, was pressing it. The SMC watches heat and power on every Mac, whatever operating system is running, and when it decides things are getting out of hand its last resorts are sleep and then a hard power-off. Something about starting a game was spooking it.

First guess: too much power

The iMac Pro's Radeon Pro Vega can draw up to 210 W, and macOS caps it when the SMC asks. Linux didn't know how to do that for Vega, so the obvious theory was that the GPU was simply drawing too much.

So we capped it, first at 150 W. The Mac still went to sleep, and still switched off. The logs even showed the GPU drawing only 50 to 86 W in the minute before one power-off. Average power wasn't the problem.

Logging everything

To stop guessing, we wrote a logger that records every SMC power zone and temperature, the GPU's own sensors and its current clock speed, written to disk line by line so it survives a power cut. Then I played a lot of very short games of CS2: eight logged runs in all.

The pattern that came out wasn't heat or average power. It was a sudden jump from idle to full load. Clicking find match, or changing the graphics preset, makes the game reload: the GPU drops to idle for a moment, then slams straight back to 100%. At the GPU's top clock speeds, that jump is what the SMC didn't like.

And the heat that follows is fast. In one run the GPU went from 52 °C to 101 °C in four seconds. The fan control only checked temperatures every two seconds and ramped up gently, so by the time the fans reached full speed the GPU was already at 100 °C.

Fix one: hold the top clocks back

The Vega has eight clock steps, from 852 MHz up to 1350 MHz on a Vega 64. Taking away the top ones made the difference:

  • Allowing steps 0 to 5 (up to 1269 MHz): the Mac switched off twice.
  • Allowing steps 0 to 4 (up to 1201 MHz): it survived repeated graphics preset changes, the worst trigger we found.

So on the iMac Pro, VorT2x's power daemon (t2-zone-power) now holds the GPU at steps 0 to 4. That's about 11% off the very top clock. In practice the cost is smaller than it sounds: once it's hot, the Vega already spends most of its time between 1.0 and 1.15 GHz anyway.

There was one catch. A GPU reset, for example after waking from sleep, keeps the manual mode but quietly forgets the limit, so a one-off setting at boot isn't enough. The daemon checks four times a second and puts the limit back if it's gone. In one test the limit dropped for a single quarter-second sample as the game started, and was back within the same second.

On its own, though, this still wasn't enough. With the limit in place, the SMC put the Mac to sleep 14 seconds into a game, while the fans were still catching up.

Fix two: fans first, not fans later

The answer was to stop waiting for the heat. VorT2x's fan control (t2-fancontrol) now also watches how busy the GPU is. When it reads 80% or more twice in a row, a quarter of a second apart, the fans go straight to full speed, with no gentle ramp. They stay there until 30 seconds after the GPU last looked busy, then the normal curve takes over again.

Needing two readings in a row means a single spike on the desktop doesn't spin the fans up. In our logs, browsing and video peaked at 27 to 49% busy, while a game sits at 100%.

The difference shows clearly. The fans were told to go to full speed half a second after the game got busy, and got there about two seconds later. The GPU then took twelve seconds to reach 97 °C, instead of four seconds to pass 100 °C. That buys the SMC the time it needs.

Together, it works

With both changes installed:

  • three and a half minutes straight into full load: no sleep, no power-off;
  • a full CS2 match, about 13 minutes at full GPU load: no sleep, no power-off, and the GPU never went above step 4.

The peak temperatures and power in that match were the same as in the runs that went to sleep. What changed is how quickly the fans got there.

Want to check it's working on your iMac Pro? Run journalctl -b -u t2-zonepowerd | grep Vega and you should see "Vega 10 graphics clock held at sclk states 0-4". Start a game and the fans should spin up within a second or two.

Tips for gaming on a T2 iMac

  • Leave fan control on Automatic. The fan boost only works in automatic mode. If you've handed the fans back to the SMC, nothing changes.
  • Play at 2560×1440, not native 5K. Every passing test was a borderless window at 2560×1440, scaled up to fill the screen. Rendering a game at 5120×2880 works the GPU much harder. In one early test at 5K, before the fan boost existed, even the clock limit wasn't enough, and we haven't tested 5K with both fixes yet.
  • Check your resolution after changing presets. In CS2, applying a graphics preset quietly set the game back to 5K.
  • Cap your frame rate if you can. With vsync off and no cap, the GPU runs flat out even in menus. A cap means less heat and quieter fans.

Which Macs get what

  • iMac Pro: the clock limit and the fan boost. This is the Mac we tested, with a Vega 64.
  • iMac 2020 (iMac20,1 and iMac20,2): the fan boost only. Their Radeon Pro 5000-series GPUs weren't part of this problem, but fans that react before the heat builds should help them too. We haven't tested one yet, so we'd love to hear from you.
  • MacBook Pros with an AMD GPU: no change, on purpose. Full fans in every game would just be noise on a laptop.

Quieter desktops, too

While reading all those logs, I noticed the iMac Pro's journal filling up with noise from hardware it doesn't have. VorT2x was installing three MacBook services on every T2 Mac:

  • The battery service found no battery, gave up, and was restarted every 10 seconds: 138 restarts in under half an hour.
  • Touch ID complained every second that it couldn't reach a sensor that isn't there.
  • Force click looked for a built-in trackpad every five seconds.

On the iMac, iMac Pro, Mac mini and Mac Pro, VorT2x now skips all three, and removes them if an earlier install added them. The SMC Control app stays, because its fan, temperature and power readings are just as useful on a desktop. MacBooks install exactly as before.

A note on login: if VorT2x turned on fingerprint login for Touch ID, removing Touch ID turns it off again. If Fedora turned it on itself when you installed it, it stays on, which is harmless without a fingerprint reader. Either way, your password works as it always has.

Getting it

All of this is out now in VorT2x v0.2.3. Already running VorT2x? Open VorT2x Update to get it. New installs get it straight away. There's nothing to switch on. The details, including every test with its settings and result, are in the pull requests for the clock limit, the fan boost and the desktop services.

There's more to try. We'd like to see how far 5K gaming can go now that the fans react in time, and to hear how the 2020 iMacs get on. If you game on a T2 iMac, tell us how it goes on GitHub.
